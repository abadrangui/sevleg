Mongolian First Hair-Cutting Ceremony: Daah UrgeehJune 6, 2024 September 3, 2026 
The Mongolian first hair-cutting ceremony is a joyful family milestone often called daah urgeeh. Relatives gather to cut the child’s long early hair in small turns, offer blessings and share a meal. The event celebrates growth, health and the child’s place within an extended family.
Names, ages and procedures vary by region and household. Some families follow an astrological calendar closely; others choose a convenient date and keep only the parts that feel meaningful.
What does daah urgeeh mean?
Daah refers to the child’s early hair, while the ceremony marks its first formal cutting. English translations include “first haircut feast” and “hair-cutting ceremony.” It is not simply a visit to a barber: each cut is paired with goodwill from a relative or guest.
When is the ceremony held?
The ceremony is usually held in early childhood. Traditional accounts often give different ages for boys and girls, but practice is not consistent enough to treat one age rule as universal. Families may consult a lama or calendar for an auspicious day, then adjust for travel, school and the availability of relatives.
Preparing the child and home
The child is dressed neatly, sometimes in a new deel. The family prepares milk tea, dairy foods, dumplings, meat and sweets according to its means. Scissors are cleaned and handled by an adult, and a container or blue khadag may be used to collect the locks.
Who cuts the first lock?
An honored elder or a person chosen as a good example may begin. Other relatives then cut small locks in turn. The order can reflect age and kinship. Guests speak a blessing, give a small present or money and touch the child affectionately.
Blessings and gifts
Common wishes concern a long life, a clear mind, good character and strong relationships. Gifts may include clothing, toys, books, money or livestock in pastoral families. The value of the gift matters less than the relationship it represents.
What happens to the hair?
Some families preserve the locks in a cloth or keepsake; others dispose of them according to local custom. Because hair can carry personal and spiritual meaning, guests should not take it or make jokes about it without permission.
The family feast
After the cutting, people eat, talk and take photographs. The atmosphere is generally warm rather than solemn. For families whose relatives live far apart, the occasion also serves as a reunion and a chance for a child to learn who belongs to the wider kin group.
How the tradition is changing
Urban families may hold the event in an apartment or restaurant, use an electric clipper after the symbolic cuts, and invite a smaller group. Video calls allow distant relatives to participate. These changes keep the social purpose of the ceremony even when the setting is new.
Safety and comfort
A symbolic tradition still requires ordinary child safety. Use clean, suitable scissors; keep blades away from the ears and eyes; allow breaks; and stop if the child is distressed. A professional can finish the haircut after relatives have made their ceremonial cuts.
Etiquette for guests
Wait for the host to invite you to cut a lock.
Offer a short, sincere blessing rather than imitating words you do not understand.
Give scissors and gifts with the right hand or both hands.
Ask before posting images of the child online.
Follow the parents’ safety instructions.
Frequently asked questions
Is daah urgeeh a religious ceremony?
It can include Buddhist or folk-religious ideas, but many families celebrate it mainly as a cultural and family event.
Must every guest cut the hair?
No. Participation depends on the hosts and the child’s comfort.
What should I give?
A modest child-appropriate gift or money is common. Ask the parents when in doubt.
